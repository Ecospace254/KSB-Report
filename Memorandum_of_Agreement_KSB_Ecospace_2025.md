## 1.1 Context and Industry Background

The world as we know it today is fast-paced; changes, alterations, and transformations happen every day, thus making adaptation critical. The global population continues to grow and resources are becoming more exhausted; food security is becoming a major challenge to address for many nations, Kenya not exempted. Farmers are faced with the challenge of producing more with less. Data-driven agriculture and adoption of new-generation technologies offer a solution to these challenges.

Kenya's sugar industry occupies a position of exceptional economic and social importance within the national agricultural framework. According to the Kenya Sugar Board and corroborated by United States Department of Agriculture reporting, the industry supports approximately **eight million Kenyans** through direct and indirect employment pathways, with the sugar sub-sector contributing substantially to agricultural gross domestic product and providing the primary income source for hundreds of thousands of smallholder farming households.

## 1.2 The Challenge: Limitations of Traditional Census Methodologies

The Crops Act No. 16 of 2013 mandates Kenya Sugar Board to devise and maintain a system that regularly obtains data on current and future production and market information of scheduled crops and also provide advice to the National Government on sugarcane security matters.

Sugarcane mapping and forecasting in Kenya has traditionally been undertaken using manual methods. Although acceptable by the geospatial fraternity, these approaches are tedious and associated with significant disadvantages:

- **Subjectivity** in area and yield estimation
- **Inadequate precision** in field boundary delineation
- **Logistical burden** of deploying hundreds of enumerators
- **High costs** for retaining officers in the fields
- **Extended timelines** that the exercise takes to complete
- **Inaccuracy** in real-time representation
- **Limited accessibility** to certain areas in the landscape

Physical methods for such mapping approaches only capture spatial extent for accessible areas. There are no comprehensive maps showing the sugarcane cropped fields and/or harvest mode in Kenya, and this constrains planning for farm inputs such as fertilizer distribution by the government.

## 1.3 The Solution: Drone and Satellite Technology

The use of drones for scientific research and data collection is becoming increasingly popular due to the numerous benefits they offer. Drones are Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS) equipped with cameras, sensors, and other instruments that can be used to collect data from the environment. Many organizations are embracing drone technology for agriculture data collection due to their efficiency and affordability.

Drone-captured spatial images provide environmental information with complete spatial and temporal resolution. In Kenya where sugarcane fields are small, a multispectral high-resolution drone image of **5 centimeters ground sample distance** can be used in:

- Locating sugarcane fields with precision
- Computing different vegetation indices to facilitate exploration of spatial variability
- Minimizing inaccuracies by capturing temporal series of images
- Distinguishing fields with sugarcane from other crops through trained AI models

Multispectral drone images are sensitive to land surface components (water, vegetation, soil) which can be used to map land cover, subsequently describing surface type and conditions at every point in space on a regular basis. Imagery data, when converted into useful information and integrated with data from Geographic Information Systems (GIS), can increase accuracy in monitoring spatial and temporal dynamics as well as crop development, and provide precise area measurements.

## 1.5 Scientific Foundation and International Context

The methodological approach employed draws upon an extensive body of peer-reviewed research demonstrating the effectiveness of remote sensing and machine learning for agricultural monitoring applications:

- A systematic review published in **Remote Sensing journal (February 2024)** consulted 1,398 scholarly papers and focused detailed analysis on 72 publications examining sugarcane yield estimation using remote sensing approaches, concluding that this represents a promising and increasingly accurate approach.

- Research presented at the **International Society for Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing Technical Commission I Mid-term Symposium (Changsha, China, May 2024)** demonstrated sugarcane recognition accuracies of **91.4%** with misrecognition rates as low as **2.8%**.

- **Australian research** on sugarcane yield prediction achieved R² values of **0.96** using advanced machine learning architectures.

- Studies from **Sri Lanka** reported R² values of **0.91** for Random Forest-based sugarcane yield prediction.

The R² = 0.923 achieved by Ecospace in Kenya compares favorably with these international benchmarks, demonstrating that Kenya-specific models can match global best practice performance.

# 3. OBJECTIVES OF THE MOA

## 3.1 Main Objective

To establish a structured, transparent, and mutually beneficial framework and long-term partnership for cooperation in promoting and building knowledge in geospatial technologies through research, education, training, outreach, and consultancy in the areas of sugarcane mapping, remote sensing, yield prediction, digitization of field records, and staff capacity building, in alignment with the objectives of:

- The Kenya Sugar Industry Strategic Plan
- Kenya Vision 2030
- The Agricultural Sector Transformation and Growth Strategy (ASTGS)
- The Crops Act No. 16 of 2013
- The Sugar Act No. 11 of 2024

## 3.2 Specific Objectives

This MOA sets the foundation of long-term cooperation between KSB and Ecospace to build mutual capacity in the use of Earth Observation (EO) and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) knowledge and enhance uptake of geospatial technology. The specific objectives are:

**(a)** To develop and continuously improve a yield prediction model achieving accuracy of R² ≥ 0.90 for spatial imagery data processing and analysis.

**(b)** To build human resource capacity through modern hands-on geospatial skills and competencies training for KSB officers.

**(c)** To set up drone-enabled data collection infrastructure at the Board.

**(d)** To establish comprehensive farm digitization covering all registered sugarcane farmers.

**(e)** To create spatially explicit tools providing reliable and precise information on area under sugarcane and location of sugarcane fields.

**(f)** To eliminate the documented 16.99% census overestimation through objective drone and satellite measurements.

**(g)** To support mill planning and supply optimization through accurate cane availability forecasting.

**(h)** To institutionalize modern digital agriculture frameworks aligned with national strategies.
